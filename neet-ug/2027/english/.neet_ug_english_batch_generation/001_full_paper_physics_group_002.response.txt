The vertical piezometer tubes indicate a height difference \(\Delta h = 15\text{ cm} = 0.15\text{ m}\) between the water columns. The density of water is \(\rho = 1000\text{ kg m}^{-3}\) and \(g = 10\text{ m s}^{-2}\).</p>
<p><strong>Step 2: Assumptions and Sign Conventions</strong><br/>Water is treated as an ideal, incompressible, non-viscous fluid in steady streamline flow. Since the pipe is horizontal, the gravitational potential energy per unit volume along the central streamline is the same at both sections.</p>
<p><strong>Step 3: Relevant Formulae and Principles</strong><br/>By the equation of continuity, \(A_1 v_1 = A_2 v_2 = Q\), where \(Q\) is the volume flow rate. By Bernoulli's principle for a horizontal pipe, \(P_1 + \frac{1}{2}\rho v_1^2 = P_2 + \frac{1}{2}\rho v_2^2\). The static pressure difference is related to the piezometer height difference by \(P_1 - P_2 = \rho g \Delta h\).</p>
<p><strong>Step 4: Step-by-Step Working</strong><br/>From continuity, \(v_2 = \left(\frac{A_1}{A_2}\right)v_1 = \left(\frac{4}{2}\right)v_1 = 2v_1\). Substituting into Bernoulli's equation gives:
\[\frac{1}{2}\rho (v_2^2 - v_1^2) = P_1 - P_2 = \rho g \Delta h\]
Dividing both sides by \(\rho\):
\[\frac{1}{2}\left((2v_1)^2 - v_1^2\right) = g \Delta h \implies \frac{3}{2}v_1^2 = 10 \times 0.15 = 1.5\text{ m}^2\text{ s}^{-2}\]
Solving for \(v_1\):
\[v_1^2 = 1.0\text{ m}^2\text{ s}^{-2} \implies v_1 = 1.0\text{ m s}^{-1}\]
The volume flow rate is:
\[Q = A_1 v_1 = (4 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^2)(1.0\text{ m s}^{-1}) = 4 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^3\text{ s}^{-1} = 400\text{ cm}^3\text{ s}^{-1}\]</p>
<p><strong>Step 5: Check and Final Answer</strong><br/>Checking units: \(\text{m}^2 \times \text{m s}^{-1} = \text{m}^3\text{ s}^{-1}\), and \(1\text{ m}^3 = 10^6\text{ cm}^3\), so \(4 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^3\text{ s}^{-1} = 400\text{ cm}^3\text{ s}^{-1}\). The flow speed increases in the narrower section as pressure drops, which is physically consistent. Thus, the volume flow rate is \(400\text{ cm}^3\text{ s}^{-1}\).</p>]]></text></generalfeedback>

  <name><text>[ch007-unit-7-properties-of-solids-and-liquids] NEET_UG_PHYSICS_PROPERTIES_OF_SOLIDS_AND_LIQUIDS_MCQ_Q02 - Coalescence of two soap bubbles in vacuum</text></name>
  <questiontext format="html"><text><![CDATA[<p>Two soap bubbles of radii \(3\text{ cm}\) and \(4\text{ cm}\) coalesce isothermally in vacuum to form a single spherical soap bubble. What is the radius of the resulting soap bubble?</p>]]></text></questiontext>
  <generalfeedback format="html"><text><![CDATA[<p><strong>Step 1: Given Data and Conditions</strong><br/>Radius of the first soap bubble, \(r_1 = 3\text{ cm}\). Radius of the second soap bubble, \(r_2 = 4\text{ cm}\). The coalescence occurs isothermally in vacuum.</p>
<p><strong>Step 2: Assumptions and Sign Conventions</strong><br/>The gas inside each bubble behaves as an ideal gas at constant temperature \(T\). Because the bubbles are in vacuum, the external pressure is zero, so the pressure inside a soap bubble of radius \(r\) is solely the excess pressure due to its two liquid-vapor surfaces.</p>
<p><strong>Step 3: Relevant Formulae and Principles</strong><br/>The pressure inside a soap bubble of radius \(r\) in vacuum is \(P = \frac{4S}{r}\), where \(S\) is the surface tension of the soap solution. By conservation of the total number of moles of gas during isothermal coalescence, \(P_1 V_1 + P_2 V_2 = P V\), where \(V = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3\).</p>
<p><strong>Step 4: Step-by-Step Working</strong><br/>Substituting \(P = \frac{4S}{r}\) and \(V = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3\) into the ideal gas mole-conservation equation:
\[\left(\frac{4S}{r_1}\right)\left(\frac{4}{3}\pi r_1^3\right) + \left(\frac{4S}{r_2}\right)\left(\frac{4}{3}\pi r_2^3\right) = \left(\frac{4S}{R}\right)\left(\frac{4}{3}\pi R^3\right)\]
Cancelling the common factor \(\frac{16\pi S}{3}\) from all terms yields:
\[r_1^2 + r_2^2 = R^2\]
Substituting \(r_1 = 3\text{ cm}\) and \(r_2 = 4\text{ cm}\):
\[R = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 5\text{ cm}\]</p>
<p><strong>Step 5: Check and Final Answer</strong><br/>The total surface area \(8\pi R^2\) equals \(8\pi (r_1^2 + r_2^2)\), which also satisfies conservation of surface energy in vacuum. The resulting radius is \(5\text{ cm}\).</p>]]></text></generalfeedback>

  <generalfeedback format="html"><text><![CDATA[<p><strong>Step 1: Given Data and Conditions</strong><br/>Three rods of equal length \(L\) and equal cross-sectional area \(A\) meet at junction \(J\). Rod with conductivity \(k_1 = 2K\) has its free end at \(T_1 = 120^\circ\text{C}\). Rod with conductivity \(k_2 = K\) has its free end at \(T_2 = 0^\circ\text{C}\). Rod with conductivity \(k_3 = 3K\) has its free end at \(T_3 = 20^\circ\text{C}\).</p>
<p><strong>Step 2: Assumptions and Sign Conventions</strong><br/>At steady state, the temperature \(T_J\) at junction \(J\) is constant in time, and no heat is lost through the sides of the rods. By conservation of energy, the net rate of heat flow into junction \(J\) must be zero.</p>
<p><strong>Step 3: Relevant Formulae and Principles</strong><br/>According to Fourier's law of heat conduction, the steady-state heat current through a rod of thermal conductivity \(k\), cross-sectional area \(A\), and length \(L\) is \(H = \frac{k A \Delta T}{L}\). At the junction, \(\sum H_{\text{into } J} = 0\).</p>
<p><strong>Step 4: Step-by-Step Working</strong><br/>Equating the sum of heat currents flowing into junction \(J\) to zero:
\[\frac{2K A}{L}(120 - T_J) + \frac{K A}{L}(0 - T_J) + \frac{3K A}{L}(20 - T_J) = 0\]
Dividing throughout by the positive non-zero factor \(\frac{K A}{L}\):
\[2(120 - T_J) + (0 - T_J) + 3(20 - T_J) = 0\]
Expanding the terms:
\[240 - 2T_J - T_J + 60 - 3T_J = 0\]
\[300 - 6T_J = 0 \implies 6T_J = 300 \implies T_J = 50^\circ\text{C}\]</p>
<p><strong>Step 5: Check and Final Answer</strong><br/>The junction temperature \(50^\circ\text{C}\) lies between the highest end temperature (\(120^\circ\text{C}\)) and the lowest (\(0^\circ\text{C}\)). Heat flows from the \(120^\circ\text{C}\) end into \(J\) at a rate proportional to \(2(120 - 50) = 140\), and leaves \(J\) toward the \(0^\circ\text{C}\) and \(20^\circ\text{C}\) ends at rates proportional to \(1(50 - 0) = 50\) and \(3(50 - 20) = 90\), respectively; indeed \(50 + 90 = 140\). Thus, the steady-state temperature of junction \(J\) is \(50^\circ\text{C}\).</p>]]></text></generalfeedback>

  <questiontext format="html"><text><![CDATA[<p>Given below are two statements regarding a uniformly charged thin spherical shell of radius \(R\) carrying a total charge \(+Q\):</p>
<p><strong>Statement I:</strong> The electric field at every point inside the spherical shell is zero.</p>
<p><strong>Statement II:</strong> The electrostatic potential at every point inside the spherical shell is zero.</p>
<p>In the light of the above statements, choose the most appropriate answer from the options given below:</p>]]></text></questiontext>
  <generalfeedback format="html"><text><![CDATA[<p><strong>Step 1: Conceptual Principle</strong><br/>By Gauss's law, the electric flux through a closed surface is \(\oint \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{A} = \frac{q_{\text{enc}}}{\varepsilon_0}\). Furthermore, the electric field is the negative gradient of the electrostatic potential, \(\vec{E} = -\nabla V\).</p>
<p><strong>Step 2: Evaluation</strong><br/>For any concentric spherical Gaussian surface of radius \(r &lt; R\) inside the thin spherical shell, all charge resides on the surface at radius \(R\), so the enclosed charge is \(q_{\text{enc}} = 0\). By spherical symmetry, the electric field inside is zero everywhere. Hence, the first claim is correct.</p>
<p><strong>Step 3: Application</strong><br/>Since \(\vec{E} = 0\) everywhere inside the shell, the potential gradient is zero, meaning the electrostatic potential \(V\) is constant throughout the interior and equals the potential on the surface: \(V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{Q}{R} \neq 0\) (with zero potential at infinity). Therefore, the second claim is incorrect.</p>
<p><strong>Step 4: Final Conclusion</strong><br/>The first statement is correct, whereas the second statement is incorrect.</p>]]></text></generalfeedback>

The entire network is connected across a \(V = 10\text{ V}\) ideal battery.</p>
<p><strong>Step 2: Assumptions and Sign Conventions</strong><br/>All capacitors are initially uncharged, and the connecting wires have zero resistance. The system is allowed to reach electrostatic equilibrium.</p>
<p><strong>Step 3: Relevant Formulae and Principles</strong><br/>For capacitors in series, \(\frac{1}{C_{12}} = \frac{1}{C_1} + \frac{1}{C_2}\). For capacitors in parallel, \(C_{\text{eq}} = C_{12} + C_3\). The total electrostatic energy stored in an equivalent capacitance \(C_{\text{eq}}\) at potential difference \(V\) is \(U = \frac{1}{2} C_{\text{eq}} V^2\).</p>
<p><strong>Step 4: Step-by-Step Working</strong><br/>First, calculate the equivalent capacitance of the series pair:
\[C_{12} = \frac{C_1 C_2}{C_1 + C_2} = \frac{3 \times 6}{3 + 6} = \frac{18}{9} = 2\ \mu\text{F}\]
Next, combine \(C_{12}\) in parallel with \(C_3\):
\[C_{\text{eq}} = C_{12} + C_3 = 2\ \mu\text{F} + 4\ \mu\text{F} = 6\ \mu\text{F} = 6 \times 10^{-6}\text{ F}\]
Now compute the total stored energy:
\[U = \frac{1}{2} C_{\text{eq}} V^2 = \frac{1}{2} (6 \times 10^{-6}\text{ F})(10\text{ V})^2 = 3 \times 10^{-6} \times 100 = 300 \times 10^{-6}\text{ J} = 300\ \mu\text{J}\]</p>

  <name><text>[ch011-unit-11-electrostatics] NEET_UG_PHYSICS_ELECTROSTATICS_MCQ_Q13 - Work done moving charge between two charged rings</text></name>
  <questiontext format="html"><text><![CDATA[<p>Two identical thin circular rings, each of radius \(R\), are placed coaxially at a separation \(R\) between their centers. They carry uniformly distributed charges \(+Q\) and \(-Q\), respectively. What is the work done by an external agent in slowly moving a small point charge \(q_0\) from the center of the negatively charged ring to the center of the positively charged ring?</p>]]></text></questiontext>
  <generalfeedback format="html"><text><![CDATA[<p><strong>Step 1: Given Data and Conditions</strong><br/>Two coaxial thin rings of radius \(R\) are separated by a distance \(d = R\) along their common axis. Ring 1 carries uniform charge \(+Q\) and Ring 2 carries uniform charge \(-Q\). A point charge \(q_0\) is moved slowly from the center \(O_2\) of the \(-Q\) ring to the center \(O_1\) of the \(+Q\) ring.</p>
<p><strong>Step 2: Assumptions and Sign Conventions</strong><br/>The process is quasi-static (slow), so the change in kinetic energy of \(q_0\) is zero. Electrostatic potential is taken to be zero at infinity. By the superposition principle, the total potential at any point is the algebraic sum of the potentials due to each ring.</p>
<p><strong>Step 3: Relevant Formulae and Principles</strong><br/>The electrostatic potential on the axis of a uniformly charged ring of radius \(R\) and charge \(q\) at a distance \(x\) from its center is:
\[V(x) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{\sqrt{R^2 + x^2}}\]
The work done by an external agent in moving \(q_0\) from \(O_2\) to \(O_1\) without change in kinetic energy is \(W_{\text{ext}} = q_0 (V_{O_1} - V_{O_2})\).</p>
<p><strong>Step 4: Step-by-Step Working</strong><br/>At \(O_1\) (center of the \(+Q\) ring, where \(x_1 = 0\) and \(x_2 = R\)):
\[V_{O_1} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left(\frac{+Q}{R} + \frac{-Q}{\sqrt{R^2 + R^2}}\right) = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 R}\left(1 - \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\]
At \(O_2\) (center of the \(-Q\) ring, where \(x_1 = R\) and \(x_2 = 0\)):
\[V_{O_2} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left(\frac{+Q}{\sqrt{R^2 + R^2}} + \frac{-Q}{R}\right) = -\frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 R}\left(1 - \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\]
Therefore, the potential difference is:
\[V_{O_1} - V_{O_2} = \frac{2Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 R}\left(1 - \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right) = \frac{Q(\sqrt{2} - 1)}{2\sqrt{2}\pi\varepsilon_0 R}\]
Multiplying by \(q_0\) gives the external work done:
\[W_{\text{ext}} = \frac{q_0 Q (\sqrt{2} - 1)}{2\sqrt{2}\pi\varepsilon_0 R}\]</p>
<p><strong>Step 5: Check and Final Answer</strong><br/>Since \(O_1\) is closer to \(+Q\) and \(O_2\) is closer to \(-Q\), \(V_{O_1} &gt; V_{O_2}\), so moving a positive \(q_0\) from \(O_2\) to \(O_1\) requires positive external work, consistent with \(\sqrt{2} - 1 &gt; 0\). Thus, the work done is \(\frac{q_0 Q(\sqrt{2} - 1)}{2\sqrt{2}\pi\varepsilon_0 R}\).</p>]]></text></generalfeedback>
