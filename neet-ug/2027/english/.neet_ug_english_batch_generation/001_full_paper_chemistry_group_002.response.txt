\(E^\circ_1 = E^\circ_{\mathrm{Cu^{2+}/Cu}} = +0.34\text{ V}\) for \(\mathrm{Cu^{2+}} + 2\mathrm{e^-} \rightarrow \mathrm{Cu}\) (\(n_1 = 2\))<br/>
\(E^\circ_2 = E^\circ_{\mathrm{Cu^{2+}/Cu^+}} = +0.15\text{ V}\) for \(\mathrm{Cu^{2+}} + \mathrm{e^-} \rightarrow \mathrm{Cu^+}\) (\(n_2 = 1\)).</p>
<p><strong>Step 2: Assumptions and Sign Conventions</strong><br/>Standard electrode potential \(E^\circ\) is an intensive property and is not directly additive. However, standard Gibbs energy change \(\Delta G^\circ\) is an extensive state function and can be added or subtracted when half-reactions are combined.</p>
<p><strong>Step 3: Relevant Formulae and Principles</strong><br/>The relationship between standard Gibbs energy change and standard electrode potential is:<br/>
\[\Delta G^\circ = -nFE^\circ\]
where \(n\) is the number of moles of electrons transferred and \(F\) is the Faraday constant.</p>
<p><strong>Step 4: Step-by-Step Working</strong><br/>Write the target half-reaction \(\mathrm{Cu^+} + \mathrm{e^-} \rightarrow \mathrm{Cu}\) (\(n_3 = 1\)) by subtracting the second half-reaction from the first:<br/>
\[(\mathrm{Cu^{2+}} + 2\mathrm{e^-} \rightarrow \mathrm{Cu}) - (\mathrm{Cu^{2+}} + \mathrm{e^-} \rightarrow \mathrm{Cu^+}) \implies \mathrm{Cu^+} + \mathrm{e^-} \rightarrow \mathrm{Cu}\]
Applying Hess's law to \(\Delta G^\circ\):<br/>
\[\Delta G^\circ_3 = \Delta G^\circ_1 - \Delta G^\circ_2\]
\[-n_3 F E^\circ_3 = -n_1 F E^\circ_1 - (-n_2 F E^\circ_2)\]
Dividing throughout by \(-F\):<br/>
\[1 \times E^\circ_3 = (2 \times 0.34) - (1 \times 0.15) = 0.68 - 0.15 = +0.53\text{ V}\]</p>
<p><strong>Step 5: Check and Final Answer</strong><br/>Since \(E^\circ_{\mathrm{Cu^+/Cu}} (+0.53\text{ V}) &gt; E^\circ_{\mathrm{Cu^{2+}/Cu^+}} (+0.15\text{ V})\), \(\mathrm{Cu^+}\) is unstable in aqueous solution and disproportionates to \(\mathrm{Cu^{2+}}\) and \(\mathrm{Cu}\), which is chemically consistent.   <generalfeedback format="html"><![CDATA[<p><strong>Step 1: Conceptual Principle</strong><br/>In IUPAC nomenclature of coordination compounds, the cation is named first followed by the anion. Within an anionic coordination sphere, ligands are named in alphabetical order with multiplicative prefixes (tri-, tetra-, etc.), and the central metal name ends in "-ate" followed by its oxidation state in Roman numerals in parentheses.</p>
<p><strong>Step 2: Evaluation of Oxidation State and Ligands</strong><br/>Each potassium counter-ion carries a \(+1\) charge, so the complex anion \([\mathrm{Fe(C_2O_4)_3}]^{3-}\) carries a \(-3\) charge. Each oxalate ligand (\(\mathrm{C_2O_4^{2-}}\)) has a charge of \(-2\) and is named "oxalato". Let the oxidation state of \(\mathrm{Fe}\) be \(x\):<br/>
\[x + 3(-2) = -3 \implies x = +3\]</p>
<p><strong>Step 3: Application</strong><br/>Naming the cation "Potassium" (without a multiplicative prefix for counter-ions), followed by three oxalate ligands ("trioxalato"), and iron in an anionic complex ("ferrate(III)") gives the complete name.</p>
<p><strong>Step 4: Final Conclusion</strong><br/>The IUPAC name of \(\mathrm{K_3[Fe(C_2O_4)_3]}\) is Potassium trioxalatoferrate(III).</p>]]></generalfeedback>
