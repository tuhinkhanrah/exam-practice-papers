In the external circuit, a \(3\ \Omega\) resistor is connected in series with a parallel combination of a \(6\ \Omega\) resistor and another \(3\ \Omega\) resistor.</p>
<p><strong>Step 2: Assumptions and Sign Conventions</strong><br/>Connecting wires are assumed to have zero resistance, and the cell is ideal in series with its internal resistance \(r\). Conventional current flows from the positive terminal of the cell through the external network.</p>
<p><strong>Step 3: Relevant Formulae and Principles</strong><br/>For two resistors \(R_1\) and \(R_2\) in parallel, the equivalent resistance is \(R_p = \frac{R_1 R_2}{R_1 + R_2}\). For resistors in series, \(R_{\text{total}} = \sum R_i\). By Ohm's law for a complete circuit, the total current is \(I = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{R_{\text{ext}} + r}\), and the potential difference across the parallel branch is \(V_p = I R_p\).</p>
<p><strong>Step 4: Step-by-Step Working</strong><br/>First, compute the equivalent resistance of the \(6\ \Omega\) and \(3\ \Omega\) resistors connected in parallel:
\[R_p = \frac{6 \times 3}{6 + 3} = \frac{18}{9} = 2\ \Omega\]
Next, add the series \(3\ \Omega\) resistor to obtain the total external resistance:
\[R_{\text{ext}} = 3\ \Omega + 2\ \Omega = 5\ \Omega\]
Including the internal resistance \(r = 1\ \Omega\), the total resistance of the circuit is:
\[R_{\text{total}} = 5\ \Omega + 1\ \Omega = 6\ \Omega\]
The total current drawn from the cell is:
\[I = \frac{12\text{ V}}{6\ \Omega} = 2\text{ A}\]
The potential difference across the parallel combination is:
\[V_p = I R_p = 2\text{ A} \times 2\ \Omega = 4\text{ V}\]
Therefore, the current passing through the \(6\ \Omega\) resistor is:
\[I_6 = \frac{V_p}{6\ \Omega} = \frac{4\text{ V}}{6\ \Omega} = \frac{2}{3}\text{ A}\]</p>
<p><strong>Step 5: Check and Final Answer</strong><br/>The current through the parallel \(3\ \Omega\) branch is \(\frac{4\text{ V}}{3\ \Omega} = \frac{4}{3}\text{ A}\), and the sum \(\frac{2}{3}\text{ A} + \frac{4}{3}\text{ A} = 2\text{ A}\) matches the total current. Thus, the current through the \(6\ \Omega\) resistor is \(\frac{2}{3}\text{ A}\).</p>]]></text></generalfeedback>

  <name><text>[ch012-unit-12-current-electricity] NEET_UG_PHYSICS_Q02 - Temperature dependence of drift velocity and resistivity</text></name>
  <questiontext format="html"><text><![CDATA[<p>Given below are two statements:</p>
<p><strong>Statement I:</strong> For a metallic conductor under a constant applied electric field, the drift velocity of free electrons increases with an increase in temperature.</p>
<p><strong>Statement II:</strong> The resistivity of an intrinsic semiconductor decreases with an increase in temperature due to a rapid increase in charge carrier concentration.</p>
<p>In the light of the above statements, choose the correct answer from the options given below:</p>]]></text></questiontext>
  <generalfeedback format="html"><text><![CDATA[<p><strong>Step 1: Conceptual Principle</strong><br/>The drift velocity of free electrons in a conductor is given by \(v_d = \frac{e E \tau}{m}\), where \(e\) is the elementary charge, \(E\) is the electric field, \(\tau\) is the average relaxation time, and \(m\) is the electron mass. The electrical resistivity of a material is \(\rho = \frac{m}{n e^2 \tau}\), where \(n\) is the number density of charge carriers.</p>
<p><strong>Step 2: Evaluation</strong><br/>In a metallic conductor, the number density \(n\) of free electrons is virtually independent of temperature. When temperature rises, lattice ions vibrate with greater amplitude, increasing the frequency of electron-lattice collisions. Consequently, the relaxation time \(\tau\) decreases, which causes the drift velocity \(v_d\) to decrease under a constant electric field. Hence, the claim that drift velocity increases with temperature is false.</p>
<p><strong>Step 3: Application</strong><br/>In an intrinsic semiconductor, raising the temperature breaks more covalent bonds, causing the charge carrier concentration \(n\) to increase exponentially. Although the relaxation time \(\tau\) decreases slightly with temperature, the rapid exponential rise in \(n\) dominates the product \(n\tau\), causing the resistivity \(\rho = \frac{m}{n e^2 \tau}\) to decrease significantly. Hence, the claim regarding semiconductor resistivity is true.</p>
<p><strong>Step 4: Final Conclusion</strong><br/>The first claim regarding metallic drift velocity is incorrect, whereas the second claim regarding intrinsic semiconductor resistivity is correct.</p>]]></text></generalfeedback>

  <name><text>[ch012-unit-12-current-electricity] NEET_UG_PHYSICS_Q03 - Effective temperature coefficient of series resistors</text></name>
  <questiontext format="html"><text><![CDATA[<p>Two resistors have resistances \(R_1 = 100\ \Omega\) and \(R_2 = 300\ \Omega\) at \(0^\circ\text{C}\). Their temperature coefficients of resistance are \(\alpha_1 = 4 \times 10^{-3}\ ^\circ\text{C}^{-1}\) and \(\alpha_2 = -1 \times 10^{-3}\ ^\circ\text{C}^{-1}\), respectively. If the two resistors are connected in series, what is the effective temperature coefficient of resistance of the combination near \(0^\circ\text{C}\)?</p>]]></text></questiontext>
  <generalfeedback format="html"><text><![CDATA[<p><strong>Step 1: Given Data and Conditions</strong><br/>At \(T_0 = 0^\circ\text{C}\), \(R_1 = 100\ \Omega\) with \(\alpha_1 = 4 \times 10^{-3}\ ^\circ\text{C}^{-1}\), and \(R_2 = 300\ \Omega\) with \(\alpha_2 = -1 \times 10^{-3}\ ^\circ\text{C}^{-1}\). The resistors are connected in series.</p>
<p><strong>Step 2: Assumptions and Sign Conventions</strong><br/>The linear temperature-resistance relation \(R(T) = R_0(1 + \alpha T)\) holds near \(0^\circ\text{C}\), where \(T\) is the temperature in degrees Celsius.</p>
<p><strong>Step 3: Relevant Formulae and Principles</strong><br/>For a series combination, the total resistance at temperature \(T\) is \(R_s(T) = R_1(T) + R_2(T)\). Expressing this in the form \(R_s(T) = R_s(0)(1 + \alpha_{\text{eff}} T)\) gives the effective temperature coefficient:
\[\alpha_{\text{eff}} = \frac{R_1 \alpha_1 + R_2 \alpha_2}{R_1 + R_2}\]</p>
<p><strong>Step 4: Step-by-Step Working</strong><br/>Substitute the given resistances and temperature coefficients into the formula:
\[\alpha_{\text{eff}} = \frac{(100\ \Omega)(4 \times 10^{-3}\ ^\circ\text{C}^{-1}) + (300\ \Omega)(-1 \times 10^{-3}\ ^\circ\text{C}^{-1})}{100\ \Omega + 300\ \Omega}\]
\[\alpha_{\text{eff}} = \frac{0.4 - 0.3}{400}\ ^\circ\text{C}^{-1} = \frac{0.1}{400}\ ^\circ\text{C}^{-1} = 2.5 \times 10^{-4}\ ^\circ\text{C}^{-1}\]</p>
<p><strong>Step 5: Check and Final Answer</strong><br/>The positive coefficient of the \(100\ \Omega\) resistor slightly outweighs the negative coefficient of the \(300\ \Omega\) resistor, yielding a small positive effective coefficient with units of \(^\circ\text{C}^{-1}\). Thus, the effective temperature coefficient of resistance is \(2.5 \times 10^{-4}\ ^\circ\text{C}^{-1}\).</p>]]></text></generalfeedback>

<p><strong>Step 2: Assumptions and Sign Conventions</strong><br/>By the right-hand thumb rule, a clockwise current in the plane of the page produces a magnetic field directed into the page, whereas a counter-clockwise current produces a magnetic field directed out of the page. Let the direction into the page be positive.</p>
<p><strong>Step 3: Relevant Formulae and Principles</strong><br/>According to the Biot-Savart law, the magnetic field at the centre of a circular arc of radius \(r\) subtending an angle \(\theta\) (in radians) and carrying current \(I\) is:
\[B = \frac{\mu_0 I \theta}{4\pi r}\]
For a straight wire whose line of action passes directly through the observation point \(O\), \(\sin\alpha = 0\) for every current element, so its magnetic field contribution at \(O\) is zero.</p>
<p><strong>Step 4: Step-by-Step Working</strong><br/>Each semicircular arc subtends an angle \(\theta = \pi\) at \(O\).
For the inner semicircular arc of radius \(R\) (clockwise current, into the page):
\[B_{\text{inner}} = \frac{\mu_0 I \pi}{4\pi R} = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4R} \quad (\text{into the page})\]
For the outer semicircular arc of radius \(2R\) (counter-clockwise current, out of the page):
\[B_{\text{outer}} = \frac{\mu_0 I \pi}{4\pi (2R)} = \frac{\mu_0 I}{8R} \quad (\text{out of the page})\]
Both radial segments lie along lines passing through \(O\), so \(B_{\text{radial}} = 0\).
Since \(B_{\text{inner}} > B_{\text{outer}}\), the net magnetic field at \(O\) is:
\[B_{\text{net}} = B_{\text{inner}} - B_{\text{outer}} = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4R} - \frac{\mu_0 I}{8R} = \frac{\mu_0 I}{8R}\]
The net field points in the direction of the stronger contribution, which is into the page.</p>
<p><strong>Step 5: Check and Final Answer</strong><br/>The dimensions \(\frac{\mu_0 I}{R}\) match those of magnetic field, and the inner arc dominates because it is closer to \(O\). Therefore, the net magnetic field at \(O\) is \(\frac{\mu_0 I}{8R}\) directed into the page.</p>]]></text></generalfeedback>

  <generalfeedback format="html"><text><![CDATA[<p><strong>Step 1: Conceptual Principle</strong><br/>Magnetic materials are classified by their magnetic susceptibility \(\chi\) and response to external magnetic fields and temperature, while a moving coil galvanometer relies on the torque on a current-carrying coil in a radial magnetic field.</p>
<p><strong>Step 2: Evaluation</strong><br/>A diamagnetic substance has paired electrons, develops an induced magnetic moment opposite to the applied field, and is weakly repelled by a magnet with a small negative susceptibility. A paramagnetic substance obeys Curie's law, so its susceptibility is positive and inversely proportional to absolute temperature (\(\chi \propto \frac{1}{T}\)).</p>
<p><strong>Step 3: Application</strong><br/>A ferromagnetic substance forms magnetic domains that align strongly with an external field and exhibit hysteresis, retaining magnetization even after the magnetizing field is removed. In a moving coil galvanometer, concave pole pieces and a soft iron core create a radial magnetic field so that the plane of the coil always remains parallel to the field lines, making the deflecting torque independent of the angle and the steady deflection directly proportional to the current.</p>
<p><strong>Step 4: Final Conclusion</strong><br/>Therefore, diamagnetic behaviour corresponds to weak repulsion and small negative susceptibility, paramagnetic behaviour corresponds to inverse temperature dependence of susceptibility, ferromagnetic behaviour corresponds to hysteresis and retentivity, and the moving coil galvanometer corresponds to linear current-deflection scaling via a radial magnetic field.</p>]]></text></generalfeedback>

  <name><text>[ch016-unit-16-optics] NEET_UG_PHYSICS_Q11 - Angle of minimum deviation through a prism</text></name>
  <questiontext format="html"><text><![CDATA[<p>A glass prism has a refracting angle of \(A = 60^\circ\) and is made of a material of refractive index \(\mu = \sqrt{2}\). What is the angle of minimum deviation produced by this prism for light travelling in air?</p>]]></text></questiontext>
  <generalfeedback format="html"><text><![CDATA[<p><strong>Step 1: Given Data and Conditions</strong><br/>The refracting angle of the prism is \(A = 60^\circ\), and the refractive index of the prism material relative to air is \(\mu = \sqrt{2}\).</p>
<p><strong>Step 2: Assumptions and Sign Conventions</strong><br/>The prism is placed in air (\(\mu_{\text{air}} = 1\)), and the light ray passes symmetrically through the prism at the condition of minimum deviation.</p>
<p><strong>Step 3: Relevant Formulae and Principles</strong><br/>At minimum deviation \(\delta_m\), the refractive index of the prism is related to \(A\) and \(\delta_m\) by the prism formula:
\[\mu = \frac{\sin\left(\frac{A + \delta_m}{2}\right)}{\sin\left(\frac{A}{2}\right)}\]</p>
<p><strong>Step 4: Step-by-Step Working</strong><br/>Substitute \(A = 60^\circ\) and \(\mu = \sqrt{2}\) into the prism formula:
\[\sqrt{2} = \frac{\sin\left(\frac{60^\circ + \delta_m}{2}\right)}{\sin\left(\frac{60^\circ}{2}\right)} = \frac{\sin\left(\frac{60^\circ + \delta_m}{2}\right)}{\sin 30^\circ}\]
Since \(\sin 30^\circ = \frac{1}{2}\):
\[\sin\left(\frac{60^\circ + \delta_m}{2}\right) = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\]
Taking the principal angle in the first quadrant:
\[\frac{60^\circ + \delta_m}{2} = 45^\circ\]
\[60^\circ + \delta_m = 90^\circ \implies \delta_m = 30^\circ\]</p>
<p><strong>Step 5: Check and Final Answer</strong><br/>At minimum deviation, the angle of incidence is \(i = \frac{60^\circ + 30^\circ}{2} = 45^\circ\) and the angle of refraction is \(r = \frac{A}{2} = 30^\circ\), which satisfies \(\frac{\sin 45^\circ}{\sin 30^\circ} = \sqrt{2}\). Thus, the angle of minimum deviation is \(30^\circ\).</p>]]></text></generalfeedback>

  <questiontext format="html"><text><![CDATA[<p>In a hydrogen atom, an electron transitions from the \(n = 3\) state to the \(n = 1\) state, emitting a photon of wavelength \(\lambda_1\). In another transition from the \(n = 2\) state to the \(n = 1\) state, a photon of wavelength \(\lambda_2\) is emitted. What is the ratio \(\frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_2}\)?</p>]]></text></questiontext>
  <generalfeedback format="html"><text><![CDATA[<p><strong>Step 1: Given Data and Conditions</strong><br/>Transition 1 occurs from \(n_i = 3\) to \(n_f = 1\) with emitted wavelength \(\lambda_1\). Transition 2 occurs from \(n_i = 2\) to \(n_f = 1\) with emitted wavelength \(\lambda_2\).</p>
<p><strong>Step 2: Assumptions and Sign Conventions</strong><br/>The hydrogen atom is modelled using the Bohr model, where the Rydberg constant \(R\) is taken to be the same for both transitions.</p>
<p><strong>Step 3: Relevant Formulae and Principles</strong><br/>For an electronic transition in a hydrogen atom from a higher level \(n_i\) to a lower level \(n_f\), the wavelength \(\lambda\) of the emitted photon is given by the Rydberg formula:
\[\frac{1}{\lambda} = R\left(\frac{1}{n_f^2} - \frac{1}{n_i^2}\right)\]</p>
<p><strong>Step 4: Step-by-Step Working</strong><br/>For the transition from \(n = 3\) to \(n = 1\):
\[\frac{1}{\lambda_1} = R\left(\frac{1}{1^2} - \frac{1}{3^2}\right) = R\left(1 - \frac{1}{9}\right) = \frac{8R}{9}\]
For the transition from \(n = 2\) to \(n = 1\):
\[\frac{1}{\lambda_2} = R\left(\frac{1}{1^2} - \frac{1}{2^2}\right) = R\left(1 - \frac{1}{4}\right) = \frac{3R}{4}\]
Taking the ratio \(\frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_2} = \frac{1/\lambda_2}{1/\lambda_1}\):
\[\frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_2} = \frac{3R/4}{8R/9} = \frac{3}{4} \times \frac{9}{8} = \frac{27}{32}\]</p>
<p><strong>Step 5: Check and Final Answer</strong><br/>Since the \(n = 3 \to 1\) transition involves a larger energy difference than \(n = 2 \to 1\), its emitted wavelength \(\lambda_1\) must be shorter than \(\lambda_2\), which is consistent with \(\frac{27}{32} < 1\). Therefore, the ratio \(\frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_2}\) is \(\frac{27}{32}\).</p>]]></text></generalfeedback>

  <questiontext format="html"><text><![CDATA[<p>In a nuclear fusion reaction, two deuterons (\({}^{2}_{1}\text{H}\)) combine to form a helium nucleus (\({}^{4}_{2}\text{He}\)). If the binding energy per nucleon of \({}^{2}_{1}\text{H}\) is \(1.1\text{ MeV}\) and that of \({}^{4}_{2}\text{He}\) is \(7.0\text{ MeV}\), what is the energy released in this reaction?</p>]]></text></questiontext>
  <generalfeedback format="html"><text><![CDATA[<p><strong>Step 1: Given Data and Conditions</strong><br/>The nuclear reaction is \({}^{2}_{1}\text{H} + {}^{2}_{1}\text{H} \to {}^{4}_{2}\text{He}\). Each deuteron has mass number \(A_1 = 2\) and binding energy per nucleon \(1.1\text{ MeV}\). The helium nucleus has mass number \(A_2 = 4\) and binding energy per nucleon \(7.0\text{ MeV}\).</p>
<p><strong>Step 2: Assumptions and Sign Conventions</strong><br/>Energy released (\(Q\)-value) is positive for an exothermic reaction where the products are more tightly bound than the reactants.</p>
<p><strong>Step 3: Relevant Formulae and Principles</strong><br/>The total binding energy of a nucleus is \(B = A \times (\text{binding energy per nucleon})\). The energy released in a nuclear reaction is the difference between the total binding energy of the products and that of the reactants:
\[Q = \sum B_{\text{products}} - \sum B_{\text{reactants}}\]</p>
<p><strong>Step 4: Step-by-Step Working</strong><br/>Calculate the total binding energy of the two reacting deuterons:
\[B_{\text{reactants}} = 2 \times (2 \times 1.1\text{ MeV}) = 4.4\text{ MeV}\]
Calculate the total binding energy of the helium product nucleus:
\[B_{\text{products}} = 4 \times 7.0\text{ MeV} = 28.0\text{ MeV}\]
Subtract to find the energy released:
\[Q = 28.0\text{ MeV} - 4.4\text{ MeV} = 23.6\text{ MeV}\]</p>
<p><strong>Step 5: Check and Final Answer</strong><br/>Because \({}^{4}_{2}\text{He}\) has a much higher binding energy per nucleon than \({}^{2}_{1}\text{H}\), a large positive amount of energy is released. Thus, the energy released in the reaction is \(23.6\text{ MeV}\).</p>]]></text></generalfeedback>
